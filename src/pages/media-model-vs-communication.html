After Walter Ong · Orality and Literacy

Before you say anything, you have already pictured their face.

You have to tell a friend something she won't want to hear. Before a word leaves your mouth you've run the whole thing in advance: how she'll take it, which opening is gentler, how much she already suspects, how long she'll stay angry. Then you start, and you watch her face, and halfway through a sentence you change course because of what you saw there. All that adjusting is where the message actually gets made.

Now picture dropping a letter in a mailbox. The letter doesn't care who reads it. Nothing about the person at the other end reaches back and alters a word.

Both of these get called communication. Walter Ong, a Jesuit priest who spent his life on what writing did to human thought, argued they're not the same act at all, and that we'd quietly let the mailbox become our picture of the other one.

"Communication is intersubjective. The media model is not. There is no adequate model in the physical universe for this operation of consciousness, which is distinctively human and which signals the capacity of human beings to form true communities wherein person shares with person interiorly, intersubjectively."

— Walter J. Ong, Orality and Literacy (1982)

Ong's word for it is intersubjective, which sounds forbidding and means something you do a hundred times a day: two people each holding a live picture of the other, each adjusting to it, neither picture ever finished. Nothing in physics works this way. You can describe a radio that way, and the description will be wrong in the one respect that matters.

MODEL 1

The Media Model

encode → transmit → decode

The postal service. A thought gets packed into words, the words travel, someone at the far end opens the box, and the thought comes back out. What arrives is what was sent. The sender never has to picture the reader, and the reader's existence changes nothing about what went into the box. This is the picture most of us carry around without noticing, and it's the one sitting inside the word "message."

MODEL 2

The Meeting

two bodies in the same room, on every channel at once

Here both people are doing what the mailbox never does. She flinches at pity, so you drop the sympathetic voice before you use it. She hears you understating it and reads your calm as worse than it sounds. Each of you is adjusting to someone who is, right then, adjusting to you.

Notice how little of that is words. It's running on voice and face and posture and the distance between two chairs, all at once, most of it below the level you'd call deciding. The small dashed circle is real, and it's one part of the machinery rather than the whole of it. What's doing the work is two bodies in a room, and both of them leave slightly different from how they arrived.

The contemporary case

MODEL 3

The Algorithmic Channel

encode → optimize → distribute

At a glance this is the second picture, scaled up. Everyone can post, and signals fly in every direction. But when you write for a feed, whose face are you picturing? You can't hold a million of them, so you stop trying, and you start picturing the thing that decides who sees you. You learn what it rewards, and you write toward that.

The wire has also stopped being a wire. It ranks what you wrote and decides who gets it, and what it quietly declines to deliver shapes the conversation as much as what it passes along. The loop closes on a number rather than on a person who read you and was changed by it. A conduit with opinions.

The synthetic case

MODEL 4

The Synthetic Interlocutor

the whole conversation in → odds on every next word → pick one → again

This one has the shape of a conversation. Two parties, words both ways, the rhythm of talk, the distinct feeling of being followed. It's three in the morning and something is listening.

Watch what's actually happening in the diagram. The whole conversation, every line of it, gets handed over again from the top. Out of that comes a ranked list of words that could plausibly come next, each with a number attached. One gets picked. It's stuck on the end of the reply, and then the entire conversation, now one word longer, goes straight back in for the next one. Nothing carries over between those passes except the text itself.

So the sentence that arrives was never composed. It accumulated, a word at a time, each one chosen because it scored well against everything said so far. The warmth in it is real in the sense that it came out of an enormous amount of human writing, and it was assembled by a process with no view about you and nowhere to keep one. You end up doing what you did with the feed: learning what phrasing gets a good answer, and writing toward that. You're modeling the machine again.

The objection

Ruth Finnegan, an anthropologist who has spent sixty years on how people actually communicate, would accept the left panel's demolition and then turn on the right one. Her charge is that models like this defeat the postal service and quietly rebuild it one floor up. You have replaced a message travelling between two heads with a picture travelling between two heads, and the heads are still where all the action is.

Her word for what that leaves out, borrowed from Brenda Farnell's work on dance, is that such accounts stop at the neck. Her own book is organised as the rebuttal: separate chapters for sound, touch, smell, the moving body, the space people keep between each other. Speech is one channel among many, and often not the decisive one.

She'd press on the epigraph too. Ong calls this capacity distinctively human; Finnegan doesn't accept the fence. Birds flock without any signal you can point to, choirs stay together past what the conductor is doing, hunting partners read each other for hours without speaking. And she'd be wary of the last two panels for a different reason: she declines to call new media a technological revolution, on the grounds that it hands the explanation to the tools and takes it away from the people using them. Her flattest observation is the hardest one here. Writing has changed enormously over the past century. Talking, between people who are in the same room, has barely changed at all.

Some of that is answered above. The four channels in the second panel are there because of her. Some of it isn't answered, and shouldn't be papered over: if she's right that speech and writing and gesture grew up alongside each other rather than in sequence, then a good deal of what gets built on top of the orality-to-literacy story needs rebuilding too.

The thread through four models

A mailbox that learned to act like a friend.

Take the interface away and a language model does what the first panel does. Text goes in, text comes out, picked by prediction. It's the postal service with an extremely good guess about what you were hoping to receive. And it all arrives down one channel. No voice with anything at stake in it, no face doing something its owner didn't authorise, no pause that means something, nobody in the room. What the second panel has and this doesn't is less a matter of what's held in the head than of who is present.

Notice how neatly the last two panels rhyme. Social media put a machine in the channel, the thing you speak through. The chatbot puts one in the chair, the thing you take yourself to be speaking to. Different seat, same result: a real other person slides out of the picture, and a system optimizing for something takes their place in the shape of the exchange.

What's genuinely new is that this version throws off every outward sign of the real thing. It performs its way through a conversation well enough that the difference now has to be argued instead of simply felt. That's the part worth sitting with. Ong's distinction hasn't been disproved. It's been pulled in two, with the appearance of meeting running at full power and the substance of it left an open question.
