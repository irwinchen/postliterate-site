1 — The problem

Every earlier study confounded literacy with school.

By the 1970s a strong claim was in circulation: writing restructures thought. Havelock argued that grouping events into categories depends on having a written language to name the categories. Greenfield and Bruner held that written language pushes cognitive growth earlier and further. Olson argued that a specific logical competence — treating meaning as residing in the text — is the endpoint of development in literate cultures, and is not universal.

The evidence for all three came from comparisons between schooled and unschooled people. Nobody had tested literacy by itself, because almost nowhere on earth do people learn to read outside a classroom. Scribner and Cole put the objection plainly: none of these studies tested literacy as such.

Scholars offering these claims presented no direct evidence that individuals in literate societies actually process information differently from those in societies without literacy. Scribner & Cole, 1981 — paraphrase of the book's opening objection

Why the Vai broke the deadlock

Vai script is learned from a friend or relative, in adulthood, outside any institution, and it carries no body of knowledge you could not get by asking someone. Schooling and literacy come apart. Almost everything else about Vai life — ecology, farming, social organization, material culture — matches their unschooled, nonliterate neighbours.

1b — The place

A coastal strip on the Sierra Leone border, fifty miles northwest of Monrovia.

Vai country runs along the Atlantic shore of Cape Mount County, from the Mano River south-east toward the Liberian capital. Scribner and Cole worked in nine towns selected to represent all the chiefdoms of the county, with Robertsport as the administrative centre of the region. The 1974 Liberian Census, which fell at the end of their interview study, let them check the survey population against the county as a whole — on age, marital status and school achievement, the two matched closely.

The Vai are Muslim and their neighbours mostly are not, but on ecology, farming, social organisation and material culture the ethnographies of Liberia treat them as barely distinguishable from the peoples around them. That similarity is what makes the script, rather than everything else about Vai life, the variable worth testing.

2 — Three literacies, one people

Not one literacy. Three, each with its own script, language, teacher and purpose.

A Vai man could be literate in a syllabary for his own language, an alphabet for a sacred foreign language, an alphabet for the government's foreign language, or any combination. Roughly a third of male literates knew two scripts. Five men in the survey knew all three.

2b — The script

A syllabary invented in the 1830s, still passed hand to hand in the 1970s.

Vai script is one of the few writing systems in Africa invented from scratch rather than adapted. It ranks among advanced writing systems because it represents the sound structure of the language systematically and because its graphic symbols are original. It also did something rarer: it took root across broad sections of an agrarian population, without a state, a school system, or a body of literature to sustain it.

Understanding the script matters for the study's central finding. The specific skill that Vai literacy built — holding syllables in mind until they resolve into words — is a direct consequence of how this script is built and how badly it under-specifies the spoken language.

Where it came from

15th–16th c.

The Vai migrate from Mande country along the upper and middle Niger to the Atlantic coast. Portuguese traders reach Cape Mount by the mid-1400s and establish lasting trade.

by the 1820s

Islam is established through Vai country, with mosques and lodging for itinerant Manding teachers in practically every sizable town. The Arabic alphabet arrives with it.

1822

The Colony of Liberia is founded at Cape Mesurado, fifty miles down the coast. English-speaking colonists and Christian missionaries make contact with Vai leaders and open the first schools.

c. 1833

A small group of Vai men elaborate a graphic system with consistent phonetic rules. Two alphabets are already in use around them. What they produce is a syllabary — which is why it cannot be dismissed as a borrowed innovation.

1854

Sigismund Koelle publishes the first Vai grammar, with a printed syllabarium and a specimen of the writing. He names Dualu Bukele as the inventor, assisted by five friends.

to 1945

Court records are kept in Vai script. After the Second World War this becomes legally unacceptable; English is made mandatory for administrative and judicial matters.

1970s

20.3% of adult Vai men read and write the script, transmitted for six generations without schools or professional teachers. Its public functions are contracting as English takes over official life.

The origin story, and what to do with it

Koelle recorded Bukele's own account. Bukele told him that about fifteen years earlier a tall, venerable-looking white man in a long coat had appeared to him in a dream, saying “I am sent to you by other white men… I bring you a book.” The messenger showed him a number of signs. On waking he could not remember them all, so he gathered a group of young friends and together they made new ones.

Scribner and Cole treat this as one set of events among several. Elders in the towns tell different stories, and the evidence suggests the script may have gone through partly independent developments in different places. Massaquoi and others argue the inventors were building on a system of graphic signs already long in use among the Vai and their neighbours. English-educated Vai scholars today largely accept Koelle's version. What is not in dispute is that Vai men, working in the early nineteenth century, produced a system with consistent phonetic rules.

Why the invention is the point

Two alphabets were already circulating in Vai country when the script was devised — Roman for the colonists and traders, Arabic for the Muslim scholars. A syllabary is a different analysis of speech from an alphabet. Whatever pressure the foreign scripts created, the form of the answer was the inventors' own.

That independence is what let Scribner and Cole use it as a test case. The script was never attached to a curriculum, a canon, or a state, so it carries none of the freight that makes schooling and literacy impossible to tell apart everywhere else.

Three reasons it is hard to read

1

Tone is not written

Vowel tone changes meaning in spoken Vai. The script does not mark it. A single character can stand for several different words.

2

Vowel length varies by hand

Length is also semantically distinctive in speech. Because the script was never standardised, how it gets represented shifts from one writer to the next.

3

Nothing is segmented

Characters run across the page without spaces or divisions. A character may be a one-syllable word, one of several such words distinguished by tone, or a fragment of a longer word.

SO

Reading is done out loud

Scribner and Cole watched men decipher letters by saying strings of syllables aloud over and over, varying tone and length until they clicked into meaning. Syllables have to be held in mind until they resolve. That is the skill the auditory integration task caught.

Why men said they learned it

Asked whether knowing the script had helped them, 89% of Vai literates gave the same answer: keeping track of their own affairs. Privacy, in other words. Money came second at 16%, helping others at 14%, and a job at 6%.

The life stories bear it out. One man learned the script so he and his friends could arrange dances and send word ahead about a girl without anyone else knowing. Another learned because his father was losing his eyesight and had to hand his letters to a neighbour to read aloud, which meant family business left the family. The script is a way of having correspondence nobody else can open.

The form of a Vai letter

Letters have evolved a stable shape. A letter opens by naming who it belongs to, greets the recipient and often his household, and then — before delivering anything — announces what kind of message is coming. The formula Scribner and Cole single out is “This is your information.” Only after that does the request or the news arrive. The letter closes by saying the writer has stopped, then names him.

The authors call this move contextualisation, and it is the thing Vai literates carried into the board-game task. Asked to explain a game to someone who had never seen it, they almost always opened with a general characterisation of what the game was before describing any of it. One farmer, asked what makes a good letter, said the first job is to make the person understand he is being informed through words, so that he will put his attention there — and added that this is the correct way of writing the script. The skill was not incidental to letter writing. It was the thing being taught.

3 — Study design

Five groups, sorted by what they could read.

Assignment was made on measured reading scores, not on self-report. Because literacy functions in Vai society are carried out almost entirely by men, hypothesis testing runs on the male sample. Nonliterate women were tested as an additional comparison group.

4 — Method

They ran the standard tests first. Then they threw out the strategy.

Phase one imported the task battery that cross-cultural psychology had built to demonstrate the effects of schooling — sorting, classification, free recall, syllogisms, metalinguistic probes. If literacy did what the theorists said, Vai script literates should look like schooled men. Phase one produced almost nothing.

So Scribner and Cole inverted the procedure. Instead of working down from a developmental theory, they worked up from an ethnography: watch what Vai literates actually do with the script, break those activities into component skills, then build tasks that demand the same skills on unrelated content.

PHASE 1

Developmental paradigm

Tasks borrowed from the literature · Chapters 8–9

  • Abstraction — sorting geometric figures
    Group cards by form, colour or number; then regroup on a different dimension; then explain why. Scored for dimensions used and quality of verbal explanation.
  • Taxonomic categorisation
    Constrained classification and free sorting of objects, scored for categorical choices and cluster structure.
  • Free recall memory
    Twenty-four items from four categories, recalled in any order. Scored for amount recalled and for clustering by category.
  • Verbal logic — syllogisms
    Six problems. Scored for correct answers and for whether the reasoning stayed inside the premises or appealed to personal experience.
  • Language objectivity — the sun-and-moon question
    If everyone agreed to swap the names of the sun and the moon, could we? What would you call the thing in the night sky? What would the sky look like?
  • Metalinguistic probes
    Naming the longest word, defining words, stating what makes a spoken Vai sentence ungrammatical.
Result: literacy does not substitute for schooling. Vai script literates were not significantly different from nonliterates on any of these measures, including the sorting and reasoning tasks singled out as most sensitive to written language. School showed effects. Unschooled literacy did not. And performances on the different metalinguistic probes barely correlated with each other, which undercut the idea that they were tapping one underlying ability.
PHASE 2

Functional paradigm

Tasks built from observed practice · Chapters 10–13

  • Communication game ← from letter writing
    Learn a board game with almost no verbal explanation, then explain it — materials removed — to someone who has never seen it. Then dictate a letter explaining it to someone far away. Scored for information transmitted and for framing the message before delivering it.
  • Auditory integration ← from decoding unsegmented script
    Listen to a Vai sentence read aloud in fragments, then repeat it and answer a comprehension question. Two conditions: fragments are whole words, or fragments are bare syllables.
  • Rebus reading and writing ← from sound-symbol mapping
    Read a sentence built from pictures whose names must be heard as syllables, not as objects. Then compose one.
  • Incremental recall ← from Qur'anic memorisation
    One word, then those two, then those three, and so on for sixteen trials — the "1 + 1" method Qur'anic students described. Scored three ways: most words recalled in any order, most recalled in correct order, and longest perfectly recalled run.
  • Grammatical rules ← from talk about writing quality
    Correct a spoken Vai error, then say what the rule is. Vai literates routinely argue about the quality of each other's writing.
Result: skills transfer, and they transfer narrowly. Every strong nonschooled effect landed on a task that mirrored a real practice. Vai script literacy showed up wherever the job resembled reading letters or writing them. Qur'anic study showed up on the memory task built from Qur'anic teaching. Neither spread beyond that.

Read the matrix in two directions

Down the school column: English schooling reaches almost everything, including the tasks built to model Vai script practice. But it is not across the board. Schooling did not improve taxonomic clustering in free recall, or flexibility in sorting, or naming the longest word, or defining words — and it did nothing for incremental recall or syllable integration. The one description that fits all its primary effects is narrower than "general cognitive growth": school trains expository talk in contrived situations.

Across the script rows: nothing spreads. Vai script literacy never reaches the general-ability tasks that schooling reaches. Qur'anic recitation reaches exactly one task, the one modelled on Qur'anic teaching. The trade is uneven — schooling captures more script-related skills than script knowledge captures school-related skills — but there are places school cannot go. On syllable integration and on incremental recall, schooling confers no advantage at all.

D · The communication game

Letter writing is the commonest use of Vai script, so the authors tested whether it built a transferable skill: explaining something to a person who cannot see what you see. Men learned a board game with minimal instruction, then explained it — game removed — first face to face, then as a dictated letter to someone far away. Vai script literates gave more game-related information and described the materials more fully than nonliterates, in both modes.

The regression settles what was doing the work. Across oral and written productions combined:

Total information = 17.2 + 2.2 (Vai reading score) + 3.5 (modernity of occupation) + 6.3 (schooling)  R² = .20

Group membership dropped out; Vai reading score replaced it. The better a man reads Vai script, the more information he transmits. Arabic literacy looked like it helped on the group means, but no Arabic variable entered the equation — occupation accounted for the apparent difference.

7 — The conclusion

Literacy as practice, not literacy as development.

A practice, in the authors' definition, is a goal-directed sequence of activities that uses a particular technology and applies a particular body of knowledge — playing the piano, sewing trousers, writing letters. Skills attach to practices. As you write more letters, the component skills get more efficient, less tied to specific content, and more available to new content. But the range of that availability is set by the range of the practice.

Vai literacy is restricted. Letters, ledgers, lists. So the skills it built were restricted to match. That is a claim about Vai society in the 1970s, not about writing.

Our results show that certain literacy practices among the Vai produced intellectual outcomes closely tied to those practices — and no evidence that Vai script literacy was associated with generalised competencies such as abstraction, verbal reasoning, or metalinguistic skill. Scribner & Cole — the finding, paraphrased

What the study does not license

The authors are explicit that their negative findings are bounded by the practices available to test. Where a society gives literacy many purposes, they expect the skill systems to become varied, complex and widely applicable — and at that point the functional and the general-ability accounts converge. Finding specific skills among the Vai does not rule out finding general ones elsewhere.

They also flag a gap in their own design: they never built tasks to model what advanced Qur'anic scholars do. Disputation and commentary on the Qur'an might well transfer. Their metalinguistic tasks, aimed at abstract features of language, were the wrong instrument for it.

Four claims the Vai data support

1

Reading and writing on their own did not produce the transformations attributed to literacy. Where schooling was absent, so were the effects.

2

School effects are not produced by learning to read. Nonschooled literacy yields a different pattern, so the ability to read and write cannot be what school is doing.

3

Nonschooled literacy is not inert. It builds real, transferable skills — analysing speech, instructing a stranger — that schooling did not confer.

4

The unit of analysis is the practice, not the medium. Different scripts produced different effects, and the differences map onto how each script is learned and used.

8 — Handle with care

Objections the authors raised against themselves.

The book devotes a section to the counterargument that literates were simply the brighter men to begin with — self-selection rather than consequence. Scribner and Cole answer that the selection story cannot account for the shape of the results. A general ability advantage would show up everywhere. What they found instead was each literacy registering on the specific tasks that mirrored its own practice, and nowhere else.

Neither literacy nor schooling was all-or-none

On every experimental task, including those showing the strongest Vai script effects, some nonliterates scored high and displayed the same skills as literates. No group boundary is a cognitive boundary.

Age and city life kept competing with literacy

Vai script literates averaged 49 years old; schooled men averaged 29. Urban residence, not schooling, turned out to be the main determinant of taxonomic classification. Effects reported here survive those controls; the raw group means do not always.

Effect sizes are modest

The communication regression accounts for about 20% of the variance. The incremental recall gap in the pilot is roughly two words. These are real, replicated differences, not large ones.

One qualification the book adds and later summaries usually drop

Scribner and Cole expected Qur'anic memorisation to show up as preserved serial order in recall. In the first tasks it did not, which surprised them. The effect emerged only once they rebuilt the task to match the actual teaching method — the one-word-at-a-time incremental procedure. The finding is therefore a finding about task design as much as about memory: the transfer was there, but it was invisible to a conventional recall test.
